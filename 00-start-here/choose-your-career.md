# Choose your career

Choose a starting career path by the work you want to understand and perform. Job titles can be useful search terms, but their responsibilities vary across organizations. A title alone is not enough to judge a role's scope or your readiness for it.

This guide helps learners explore role families and helps working engineers identify adjacent areas. It does not predict hiring outcomes, salaries, or local market demand. The career folders are being developed, so linked locations may still contain placeholders.

## Begin with the work

Ask what kind of problem keeps your attention. Do you enjoy making releases safer, investigating failures, building systems other engineers use, understanding traffic, protecting services, or operating data infrastructure?

Also consider the working conditions: collaboration, coding, documentation, operational ownership, customer interaction, and incident response. The same technology can appear in roles with very different daily responsibilities.

CI/CD refers to continuous integration and continuous delivery or deployment, depending on context. SRE means site reliability engineering. MLOps concerns the engineering and operational practices around machine-learning systems. These terms describe areas of work; they do not settle the scope of every job using them.

## Understand overlapping roles

DevOps work often concerns collaboration and delivery across development and operations. SRE work often emphasizes reliability and operational engineering. Platform work often supplies reusable capabilities to development teams. Cloud engineering often focuses on cloud environments and their operation.

Treat those descriptions as orientation. In one organization, a team may combine all four. Elsewhere, responsibilities may be separated. Read job descriptions and ask about actual ownership before drawing conclusions from the title.

A useful comparison asks who the role serves, what outcomes it owns, which systems it changes, how success is measured, and what happens when those systems fail.

## Assess your current foundations

Record what you can demonstrate rather than whether you have heard of a tool. Can you explain a process and a service? Trace a request across a network? Read a configuration? Make and review a version-controlled change? Write a small script? Interpret logs and errors?

Use [Browse by domain](browse-by-domain.md) to identify foundation gaps. Advanced responsibilities still rely on those foundations. If a role guide assumes a topic you do not understand, study that dependency before treating the curriculum as a checklist.

## Try a small piece of the work

Choose an isolated activity that reflects the responsibility you are evaluating. For delivery, describe and test a change path. For operations, investigate a deliberately introduced lab failure. For platform work, document a repeatable service setup and ask another learner to follow it. For data recovery, demonstrate restoring test data and checking its integrity.

Select an actual exercise only after its environment, expected results, costs, and cleanup are clear. These suggestions describe types of work, not complete execution instructions.

Afterwards, ask whether you enjoyed understanding the problem, documenting the result, and improving the procedure. Enjoying a product interface is useful, but it is not the same as enjoying ongoing ownership of the service.

## Compare two paths without committing too early

**Illustrative example:** you enjoy automation and diagnosing incidents. DevOps delivery and SRE reliability are both worth exploring. Read their responsibilities, compare the outcomes they emphasize, and try one controlled exercise from each when available. Keep common foundations while investigating the differences.

You do not need to abandon one path to learn another. Use adjacent-role material to distinguish transferable knowledge from genuinely new responsibilities.

## Create a provisional learning plan

Write down your chosen role family, the responsibilities you want to understand, your foundation gaps, and one practical outcome you intend to demonstrate. Review the plan after you have real evidence from learning or practice.

Certificates and completed courses may support learning, but assess them alongside what you can explain and demonstrate. Avoid assuming a credential replaces experience in operating a system.
